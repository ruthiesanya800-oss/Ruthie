In 2012, a man stepped off a tiny platform 39 kilometers above New Mexico and fell back to Earth so fast he broke the speed of sound. With his own body. Millions of people watched it live. And the company that paid for it doesn't make rockets, parachutes, or spacesuits. It makes a drink. A small, sweet, slightly weird-tasting drink that started out in Thailand as a cheap pick-me-up for truck drivers. Today that company sells more than 13 billion cans a year, owns two Formula 1 teams, several soccer clubs, and a media company. So how did a tonic for tired workers turn into one of the most powerful brands on the planet? Let's get into it.

To understand Red Bull, you have to start with a different bull. A red one. In Thailand.

Chaleo Yoovidhya was born in 1923 in a rural province in Thailand to a poor Chinese-Thai family. His parents raised ducks and sold fruit. Not exactly a billionaire starter pack. As a young man, Chaleo moved to Bangkok and got a job as a salesman for a pharmaceutical company. He was good at it. Really good. And at some point he thought, why am I making money for somebody else?

So in 1956 he started his own company, TC Pharmaceutical. At first it sold antibiotics and other basic medicines. But in the 1970s Chaleo noticed something. Asia was full of so-called energy tonics. Little bottles of sweet, syrupy liquid with caffeine and vitamins, sold to people who worked long, exhausting hours. In Japan, one of these drinks had made its maker one of the biggest companies in the country. Chaleo figured Thailand could have its own.

In 1976 he launched it. He called it Krating Daeng. In Thai, a krating is a gaur, a massive wild bison that lives in the jungles of Southeast Asia. Daeng means red. So, red gaur. Red bull. Hold that thought, because it comes back later.

The logo was two red bulls charging at each other in front of a yellow sun. The drink came in a small brown glass bottle. It wasn't fizzy. It was very sweet. And it was cheap. Chaleo didn't sell it to rich people in Bangkok. He sold it to the people who actually needed it. Truck drivers on night shifts. Construction workers. Farmers. People pulling 14-hour days in the heat. He even sponsored Thai boxing matches, so the fighters and the crowds would see the charging bulls everywhere. It worked. Krating Daeng became a hit across Thailand.

And for a while, that's all it was. A popular Thai drink for tired workers. It probably would have stayed that way forever if an Austrian toothpaste salesman hadn't gotten jet lag.

Enter Dietrich Mateschitz. Mateschitz was born in Austria in 1944. And if you're looking for a straight-A business genius origin story, sorry. This isn't it. He took about ten years to finish his university degree in business. Ten. He later joked that he was enjoying student life a little too much. After he finally graduated, he worked in marketing, selling everyday stuff like detergent and coffee. By the early 1980s, he was the international marketing director for a German company called Blendax. Their big product was toothpaste.

Now, here's the funny connection. Blendax had a business relationship with Chaleo's company in Thailand. So Mateschitz kept flying to Bangkok for work. And on one of those trips, around 1982, he was absolutely wrecked by jet lag. Somebody handed him a little bottle of Krating Daeng. He drank it. And his jet lag was just gone.

That same trip, Mateschitz reportedly read about a Japanese company that had become one of the biggest taxpayers in Japan just by selling energy tonics. He put those two things together. Asia was already crazy about these drinks. Europe had nothing like them. Not one. Mateschitz looked at a cheap bottle of Thai truck driver juice and saw a brand-new category. Not a new drink. A whole new shelf in the supermarket.

So he went to Chaleo with a pitch. Let's take this to the rest of the world. In 1984, the two men started a company together called Red Bull GmbH. Each of them put in around 500,000 dollars. Chaleo's family got 49 percent. Mateschitz got 49 percent. And the last 2 percent went to Chaleo's son. But here's the key part of the deal. Mateschitz would run the company. The Thai side supplied the original recipe. The Austrian side built everything else.

And Mateschitz quit his toothpaste job to do it. He was around 40 years old. Stable career, company car, good salary. All gone, for a drink nobody in Europe had heard of. That's either genius or a midlife crisis. Turns out it was both.

Mateschitz knew Europeans would not drink a flat, super sweet syrup out of a brown medicine bottle. So he changed it. He made it carbonated. He made it less sweet. And he put it in a small, skinny silver and blue can that looked nothing like a soda. It looked expensive. And he priced it like it was expensive, too. Way more than a Coke. On purpose. If it costs more, people assume it does more.

Then he did some market research. And the results were a disaster. People hated the taste. Some described it as disgusting. They didn't get the logo. They didn't understand what the drink was for. Basically every number said this product should not exist. Most CEOs would have stopped right there. Mateschitz looked at the research and decided the research was wrong.

But before he could even launch, he had another problem. Austria wouldn't approve the drink. It was full of ingredients that regulators had never seen in a soft drink before. Caffeine, sugar, B vitamins, and something called taurine. It took around three years of paperwork just to get permission to sell it in his own country.

Finally, on April 1st, 1987, Red Bull went on sale in Austria. Yes, April Fools' Day. Which honestly feels right.

To sell it, Mateschitz needed a line. He went to his old university friend, Johannes Kastner, who ran an advertising agency. Kastner reportedly came up with slogan after slogan, and Mateschitz rejected every single one. This went on for something like a year and a half. Kastner was ready to give up. Then, almost as a last try, he came back with this: Red Bull gives you wings. Mateschitz loved it. And that line has followed the brand ever since. Sounds simple, but think about it. It doesn't tell you what's in the can. It tells you how you're supposed to feel.

Now, here's where Red Bull gets weird. Most drink companies of the time marketed the same way. Big TV commercials. Billboards. Famous faces. Red Bull basically did none of that at first. Instead, it went where young people already were.

The company hired college students as brand managers. They got a car with a giant Red Bull can stuck on the back, a trunk full of free drinks, and one job. Show up wherever students were tired. Libraries during exams. Parties at 2 a.m. Ski slopes. Nightclubs. Hand out cans. For free. Let people try it at exactly the moment they needed it.

And then there's the legend. The story goes that the company would leave empty Red Bull cans in the trash bins of trendy bars and clubs, so everyone would think, wait, everybody's drinking this? Whether it happened exactly like that or not, the idea is the whole strategy. Don't tell people Red Bull is cool. Make them see it everywhere and decide for themselves.

Meanwhile, something else was helping the brand, and Red Bull didn't plan it at all. Rumors. Germany didn't approve the drink at first, so people from Germany would drive over the border to Austria just to buy it. Some people said it was basically speed in a can. Others claimed taurine came from bull testicles. It doesn't, by the way. Taurine was first discovered in ox bile back in the 1800s, and the stuff in Red Bull is made in a lab. But the rumors made the drink sound forbidden and dangerous. And if you're 19, forbidden and dangerous sounds amazing.

Red Bull grew slowly at first. Then very, very fast. It moved into Hungary and Slovenia in 1992. Germany and the UK in 1994. And in 1997, it landed in the United States, starting in California. Not everywhere welcomed it. France didn't let the original formula in until 2008. Denmark held out until 2009. Each ban just made the can a little more mysterious.

Now, here's the part where Red Bull stops acting like a drink company. Most brands buy ad space around events. Red Bull decided to own the events. In 1992, it held the first Red Bull Flugtag in Vienna. Regular people built homemade flying machines and launched them off a ramp into water. Nobody actually flew. Almost everyone crashed. Crowds loved it. Gives you wings, right? It was the slogan turned into a live show.

That set the rule for everything after. Red Bull started sponsoring athletes in sports most companies ignored. Skateboarding. Snowboarding. BMX. Cliff diving. Motocross. Base jumping. These weren't the most popular sports. They were the most extreme. And their fans were exactly the audience Red Bull wanted. Young, restless, and always looking for the next big rush.

Then the company started going bigger. Like, way bigger. In 2004, Red Bull bought the Jaguar Formula 1 team from Ford. The price was reportedly a single dollar, plus a promise to keep the team running. And the next season, Red Bull Racing was on the grid. A drink company was now competing against Ferrari and Mercedes. People laughed. Then Red Bull bought a second team, Minardi, in 2005, and renamed it Toro Rosso. Which is Italian for, you guessed it, red bull.

Hold on, because the soccer chapter is even wilder. In 2005, Red Bull bought an Austrian club in Salzburg. It changed the name, changed the colors, and changed the logo. Basically everything except the grass. A lot of the old fans were furious and started their own club instead. Then came the New York Red Bulls in 2006. And in 2009, Red Bull went to Germany. German rules ban clubs from being named after sponsors. So Red Bull found a tiny team in the fifth division and created a club called RasenBallsport Leipzig. RB Leipzig. RB. Totally a coincidence. Within about eight years, that club went from the fifth tier all the way to the top of German soccer and into the Champions League. German fans, let's say, were not big fans.

But it was working. On the track, Red Bull Racing went from a punchline to a superpower. Sebastian Vettel won four world championships in a row, from 2010 to 2013. Then Max Verstappen took four more, from 2021 to 2024. The team that people laughed at was now the team everyone else was chasing.

And then came the biggest stunt of all. Remember the man falling from space? Yeah, that one. October 14th, 2012. Red Bull Stratos. Austrian skydiver Felix Baumgartner rode a giant helium balloon up about 39 kilometers, almost 128,000 feet, above New Mexico. And this wasn't a weekend idea. The project took around five years to build, with a team of scientists, engineers, and doctors. The old record had stood since 1960, set by a US Air Force pilot named Joe Kittinger. Red Bull didn't try to hide him. It hired him. Kittinger was the voice on the radio, talking Felix through every step from the ground. One minor problem. On the way up, Felix's visor started fogging, and for a moment the whole mission almost got called off. Imagine spending five years and millions of dollars and then canceling because of foggy glasses. They pushed through. He stood at the edge of the capsule, said a few words, and stepped off. He fell for around four minutes before opening his parachute. At his top speed, he went faster than the speed of sound. More than eight million people watched the live stream on YouTube at the same time, a record back then. TV channels around the world picked it up. And the logo on his capsule, his suit, and every single camera angle? You already know.

Think about that for a second. Red Bull didn't buy an ad during the most-watched event of the year. It created the most-watched event of the year. Around the same time, the company launched Red Bull Media House, with its own magazines, films, and TV channel. At that point, Red Bull wasn't just a drink with good marketing. It was a media company that also happened to sell a drink.

Now, of course, a story this big needs at least one awkward moment. In 2014, a group of people in the US sued Red Bull over a pretty bold claim. Their argument? Red Bull says it gives you wings and improves performance, but it doesn't really do anything special. Not more than a cup of coffee, anyway. Red Bull denied doing anything wrong, but it agreed to settle for about 13 million dollars. Customers who bought Red Bull in the previous years could claim 10 dollars in cash or 15 dollars in Red Bull products. Yes, you could get compensated for Red Bull with more Red Bull. That's honestly a power move.

And here's maybe the most surprising fact in this whole story. Red Bull barely makes anything. The drink itself is produced by a separate family-owned juice company in Austria and Switzerland called Rauch. Red Bull's job is basically the brand. The events. The athletes. The image. The company has said for years that it spends roughly a third of its revenue on marketing. A third. Most companies would call that insane. Red Bull calls it Tuesday.

Over the years, both founders became billionaires. Chaleo Yoovidhya, the man who started it all with a brown bottle for truck drivers, died in 2012 as one of the richest people in Thailand. His family still owns the majority of the company.

Dietrich Mateschitz rarely gave interviews and stayed out of the spotlight, which is kind of ironic for a guy who launched a man from space. He passed away in October 2022, at 78 years old. His son, Mark Mateschitz, inherited his share and became one of the youngest billionaires in Europe overnight.

Without its founder, Red Bull has had a bumpy few years. In 2025, the company parted ways with Christian Horner, who had run its Formula 1 team for 20 years. And from 2026, Red Bull started building its own Formula 1 engines, with help from Ford. A drink company making F1 engines. Sounds made up. It isn't.

So, where does that leave Red Bull today? In 2024, it sold more than 13 billion cans in around 175 countries, with sales of over 11 billion euros. It's still the biggest energy drink in the world. It runs events on every continent, from cliff diving to air racing to break dancing. And the same skinny silver and blue can is still on the shelf, looking almost exactly like it did in 1987.

Monster, Celsius, Prime, and dozens of others have tried to take its crown. Some of them have even sold more cans in certain years. But none of them have built anything quite like the world Red Bull built around a can.

So that's the story. A poor kid in rural Thailand who became a salesman. A cheap, sweet tonic for truck drivers. A jet-lagged toothpaste marketer who couldn't stop thinking about it. A product that failed its taste tests and launched anyway. Then free cans at 2 a.m. parties, crashing homemade aircraft, two F1 teams, a soccer club nobody asked for, and a man falling from the edge of space.

In 1987, the research said nobody wanted this drink. Today, nobody can escape it. Maybe the lesson is this: the numbers can tell you what people think they want, but they can't tell you what they'll fall in love with.
